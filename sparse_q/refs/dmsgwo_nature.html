Abstract

To address the shortcomings of the Grey Wolf Optimizer (GWO) in solution accuracy, convergence speed, and search capability, this paper proposes a Dynamic Multi-Strategy Grey Wolf Optimizer (DMSGWO). Based on GWO, DMSGWO introduces four improvement strategies. First, a nonlinear convergence factor strategy is adopted to better balance global exploration and local exploitation. Second, a population dynamic grouping strategy is employed to dynamically adjust the population sizes of exploration and exploitation groups during the iteration process. Third, a random position update strategy is applied to the exploration group to enhance the global exploration capability. Finally, an adaptive perturbation position update strategy is applied to the exploitation group to improve the local exploitation capability. To comprehensively evaluate the performance of DMSGWO, it is compared with GWO, four other swarm intelligence optimization algorithms, and four other improved GWO algorithms on 23 benchmark test functions and the CEC2022 test suite. The comparative results demonstrate that DMSGWO exhibits excellent solution accuracy, stability, and convergence speed. Further validation through Friedman ranking tests and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests confirms that DMSGWO ranks first in overall performance. The test results show statistically significant performance differences from the other algorithms on most test functions, and further verify the superior performance of DMSGWO. To validate the practical application value of DMSGWO, it is applied to two typical engineering design optimization problems and WSN coverage optimization problem alongside the comparative algorithms. The results show that DMSGWO can effectively handle constraints and achieve the best objective values. Thus, these results demonstrates the effectiveness and practicality of DMSGWO in solving real-world complex application problems.

Introduction

Swarm Intelligence (SI) is a kind of meta-heuristic algorithms that solve optimization problems by simulating the collective behaviors observed in nature. These algorithms originate from observations of the phenomena and rules governing the behavior of biological groups in nature. They emphasize local interactions and information sharing among individuals and achieve global optimization through self-organization and adaptive mechanisms. Classic algorithms such as the Genetic Algorithm (GA)1, Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO)2, and Ant Colony Optimization (ACO)3 have achieved significant results. In recent years, with a deeper understanding of natural behaviors, new swarm intelligence algorithms such as the Artificial Bee Colony Algorithm (ABC)4, Firefly Algorithm (FA)5, Whale Optimization Algorithm (WOA)6, and Sparrow Search Algorithm (SSA)7 have been successively proposed and have demonstrated excellent performance in solving complex optimization problems.

Grey Wolf Optimizer (GWO)8 was proposed by Mirjalili et al. in 2014. Inspired by the social hierarchy and cooperative hunting behavior of grey wolves9, it has gained widespread attention due to its good convergence, few parameters, and easy implementation. In GWO, the convergence factor a linearly decreases from 2 to 0 to regulate the algorithm’s exploration and exploitation10. This linear change cannot flexibly adapt to different optimization stages and results in weaker search capabilities. Furthermore, during position updates in each iteration, the algorithm assigns equal weight to the three leading wolves. All individuals follow the same update strategy without establishing a clear collaboration mechanism between exploration and exploitation11. This makes the algorithm prone to premature convergence or insufficient exploitation when facing complex problems12. To address limitations such as low solution accuracy, slow convergence, and being easily trapped in local optima, researchers have proposed various improvements13. Focusing on enhancing algorithm performance, researchers mainly improve GWO from two directions. The first is designing a non-linear convergence factor to more precisely regulate the search behavior of the algorithm at different iteration stages, thereby better balancing exploration and exploitation. The second is improving the position update formula to enhance the algorithm’s search capability.

Regarding improvements to the convergence factor: Yihui Qiu et al.14 introduced a nonlinear convergence strategy. However, a single nonlinear convergence factor mainly varies with iterations and lacks real-time feedback on population stagnation and individual fitness differences. Hongbo Wang et al.15 redefined the linear parameter and combined crossover and mutation strategies to enhance the balance between exploration and exploitation. However, crossover and mutation have multiple combination forms, requiring appropriate selections for different tasks, and a unified dynamic adaptive grouping mechanism is absent.

Regarding improvements to the position update formula: Jiashuo Chen et al.16 proposed a random position update formula to reduce the wolves’ dependence on leader wolves and increase diversity, but this may cause convergence oscillations in later stages. Yufei Zhang et al.17 integrated DE-inspired mutation operators, evolutionary population dynamics, and nonlinear population size reduction into GWO, allowing the mutation process to switch between exploration and exploitation modes based on iteration probability. However, the algorithm primarily improves position updates through mutation, repositioning, and population size reduction, without directly constructing exploitation-stage perturbations based on individual fitness differences.

Although many GWO variants have been proposed to improve the original algorithm, several limitations remain insufficiently addressed. First, the linear convergence factor in GWO cannot flexibly regulate the transition between global exploration and local exploitation, which may lead to premature convergence in complex search spaces. Second, most GWO variants still update all individuals using similar leader-guided mechanisms, resulting in limited population diversity and insufficient functional differentiation among individuals. Third, although some multi-strategy GWO variants integrate multiple improvement strategies, the interaction among these strategies is often not explicitly coordinated. Therefore, the main research question of this study is how to construct a coordinated GWO framework that can dynamically balance global exploration and local exploitation. To address this question, this paper proposes a Dynamic Multi-Strategy Grey Wolf Optimizer (DMSGWO). Unlike existing GWO variants that simply combine multiple strategies, DMSGWO is designed as a coordinated and dynamically adjusted search framework. Its novelty does not lie in the independent originality of each individual strategy, but in the coordinated integration of dynamic population-level task division and nonlinear convergence regulation. Specifically, DMSGWO dynamically assigns exploration and exploitation tasks according to the change in the population best solution, while the nonlinear convergence factor regulates the transition between global exploration and local exploitation. Through this mechanism, DMSGWO establishes a clearer collaboration between global exploration and local exploitation. The main contributions are as follows:

  • Design a nonlinear convergence factor. It decreases slowly in the early stage to allow the algorithm sufficient exploration and declines rapidly in the later stage to enhance local fine-grained search. This effectively achieves a better balance between exploration and exploitation.

  • Design a dynamic population grouping strategy. The population is dynamically divided into exploration and exploitation groups, and assigns them different search tasks to establish a clear collaborative mechanism between exploration and exploitation. The sizes of the two groups are adjusted adaptively based on iterative feedback.

  • For individuals in the exploration group, design a random position update strategy. Based on random probability, the Lévy distribution is employed to achieve mutational exploration, or two randomly selected individuals are used for undirected exploration. This enhances population diversity and improves the algorithm’s ability to escape local optima.

  • For individuals in the exploitation group, design an adaptive disturbance position update strategy. Adaptive disturbance is implemented based on different individual positions, and weights are assigned according to the iteration progress to enhance the local search capability.

Grey Wolf Optimizer

Inspiration

GWO is inspired by the social hierarchy of grey wolves18. In nature, they form stable packs of 5 to 12 individuals with a strict hierarchy19, as shown in Fig. 1. Based on the different roles during hunts, they can be classified into four levels: the α wolf serve as leaders and primary decision-makers; the β wolf assist in decision-making and maintain order; the δ wolf rank below the former two but lead the ω wolf and the ordinary wolves20. This hierarchy dynamically adjusts based on individual conditions, ability, and social interactions, which reflects natural collective intelligence built on leadership, cooperation, and feedback.

GWO is also inspired by the hunting behavior of grey wolves21. When hunting, grey wolves follow three key steps: chasing, encircling, and attacking. The coordinated and adaptive strategies wolves employ during this process provide a biological model for GWO.

Encircling the prey

During hunting, grey wolves gradually approach their prey and form an encirclement22. The mathematical model is as follows:

$$D=\left| {C \cdot {X_p}(t) - X(t)} \right|$$
(1)
$$X(t+1)={X_p}(t) - A \cdot D$$
(2)

where \(\:t\) is the current iteration number, \(\:D\) is the distance between the current grey wolf and the prey, \(\:{X}_{p}\left(t\right)\) is the current prey position, \(\:X\left(t\right)\) is the current grey wolf position, \(\:X\left(t+1\right)\) is the position of the grey wolf in the next generation, and the calculation methods for the random coefficients \(\:A\) and \(\:C\) are as follows:

$$A=2 \cdot a \cdot {r_1} - a$$
(3)
$$C=2 \cdot {r_2}$$
(4)
$$a=2 - 2 \cdot \frac{t}{T}$$
(5)

Here, both \(\:{r}_{1}\) and \(\:{r}_{2}\) are random vectors uniformly distributed in the interval [0,1]. The convergence factor \(\:a\) linearly decreases from 2 to 0 as the iteration number \(\:t\) increases, where \(\:T\) denotes the maximum number of iterations.

Hunting

In the grey wolf population, hunting is jointly guided by the top three leading wolves (α, β, δ) ranked by fitness value, while other ordinary wolves update their positions based on the positions of these three leading wolves23. Its specific manifestation is illustrated in Fig. 2, and the mathematical model is as follows:

$$\left\{ \begin{gathered} {D_\alpha }=\left| {{C_1} \cdot {X_\alpha }(t) - X(t)} \right| \\ {D_\beta }=\left| {{C_2} \cdot {X_\beta }(t) - X(t)} \right| \\ {D_\sigma }=\left| {{C_3} \cdot {X_\sigma }(t) - X(t)} \right| \\ \end{gathered} \right.$$
(6)
$$\left\{ \begin{gathered} {X_1}(t+1)={X_\alpha }(t) - {A_1} \cdot {D_\alpha } \\ {X_2}(t+1)={X_\beta }(t) - {A_2} \cdot {D_\beta } \\ {X_3}(t+1)={X_\sigma }(t) - {A_3} \cdot {D_\sigma } \\ \end{gathered} \right.$$
(7)
$$X(t+1)=\frac{{{X_1}(t+1)+{X_2}(t+1)+{X_3}(t+1)}}{3}$$
(8)

Here, \(\:{A}_{1}\), \(\:{A}_{2}\) \(\:{A}_{3}\) and \(\:{C}_{1}\), \(\:{C}_{2}\), \(\:{C}_{3}\) are the coefficients for the α, β and δ wolf, respectively. \(\:{D}_{\alpha\:}\), \(\:{D}_{\beta\:}\), and \(\:{D}_{\delta\:}\) represent the distances between the current individual and the top three leading wolves, respectively. \(\:{X}_{1}\left(t+1\right)\), \(\:{X}_{2}\left(t+1\right)\), and \(\:{X}_{3}\left(t+1\right)\) denote the positions of the next-generation individual calculated based on the guidance of the top three leading wolves, respectively. \(\:X\left(t+1\right)\) is the position of the next-generation individual obtained by equally weighting the contributions from the three leading wolves.

Exploration and exploitation

In swarm intelligence optimization algorithms, exploration and exploitation are two crucial search phases that influence algorithm performance24. Exploration refers to guiding the search process to broadly explore the entire solution space to avoid overlooking potentially high-quality solutions25. And exploitation involves conducting fine-grained local searches around high-quality solutions to find better solutions26. According to Eq. (3), the parameter \(\:a\) determines the value range of \(\:A\). In other words, \(\:A\) is a random value in the interval [-\(\:a\),\(\:a\)] where \(\:a\) is decreased from 2 to 0 over the course of iterations27. In order to mathematically model divergence, random values greater than 1 or less than − 1 are used to oblige the search agent to diverge from the prey. This emphasizes exploration and allows GWO to search globally. Figure 3(a) shows that \(\:\left|A\right|>1\) forces the grey wolves to diverge from the prey to hopefully find a fitter prey. When random values of \(\:A\) are in [-1,1], the next position of a search agent can be in any position between its current position and the position of the prey. This emphasizes exploitation and guides GWO to perform local search28. Figure 3(b) also shows that \(\:\left|A\right|<1\) forces the wolves to attack towards the prey. The parameter \(\:a\) is decreased linearly from 2 to 0 in order to emphasize exploration and exploitation, respectively. Candidate solutions tend to diverge from the prey when \(\:\left|A\right|>1\) and converge towards the prey when \(\:\left|A\right|<1\). Finally, the GWO algorithm is terminated by the satisfaction of an end criterion.

Nonlinear convergence factor strategy

As a swarm intelligence optimization method, the performance of GWO depends on the regulation of the convergence factor \(\:a\) largely29. In GWO, the convergence factor \(\:a\) decreases linearly from 2 to 0 as the iteration number \(\:t\) increases30. Although this linear decrease method is simple and intuitive to implement, its relatively rapid decline in the early stage of iteration can cause the algorithm to shift from the global exploration phase to the local exploitation phase prematurely. The consequence of this is that the algorithm does not search the solution space sufficiently, easily converge to local optima prematurely, thereby reducing the possibility of finding the global optimum31. To address this issue, scholars have focused on dynamically adjusting the convergence factor \(\:a\) to better balance the algorithm’s exploration and exploitation capabilities in recent years, which has become one of the key research directions for improving GWO32. Therefore, DMSGWO designs a nonlinear convergence factor as shown in Eq. (9). Its variation trend illustrated in Fig. 4. Compared with that of GWO, the proposed nonlinear convergence factor decreases slowly in the early stage and rapidly in the later stage. This design maintains strong global exploration capability in the early stage, allowing thorough traversal of different regions of the solution space and avoiding premature convergence to local optima. In the later stage, it accelerates the decrease, prompting the algorithm to focus on promising regions and enhancing local fine-grained search, thereby achieving a better balance between global exploration and local exploitation. Its specific expression is as follows:

$$a=\frac{2}{{{{\log }_{10}}(2)}} \cdot {\log _{10}}\left( {2 - {{\left( {\frac{t}{T}} \right)}^3}} \right)$$
(9)

Population dynamic grouping strategy

In GWO, all individuals follow the same position update formula, without establishing a clear collaborative mechanism for exploration and exploitation within the population. The balance between exploration and exploitation relies solely on the single parameter \(\:a\), which can easily lead the algorithm to trap in local optima or converge slowly when tackling complex optimization problems. To more precisely and proactively coordinate exploration and exploitation, DMSGWO introduces the population dynamic grouping strategy. This strategy dynamically divides all individuals \(\:N\) into an exploration group \(\:{N}_{1}\) and an exploitation group \(\:{N}_{2}\) (\(\:{N=N}_{1}+{N}_{2}\)). The exploration group focuses on broad searches to identify potentially optimal regions and enhance the algorithm’s ability to escape local optima. And the exploitation group concentrates on fine-grained refinement around current better solutions to improve convergence accuracy and search efficiency. The sizes of the two groups are not fixed. Instead, their proportional scale is adaptively adjusted through iterative feedback to make the population structure more flexible.

The specific working mechanism is as follows: Firstly, the population is divided after random initialization such that the top 50% of individuals are assigned to the exploration group and the bottom 50% to the exploitation group. This setting allocates the same population size to exploration and exploitation at the beginning of the optimization process, thereby avoiding an initial bias toward either search behavior. Meanwhile, the two groups execute completely different position update formulas to ensure the efficiency of the grouping operation and assign distinct search tasks to each group. During iterations, the group sizes are not static but are dynamically adjusted after each iteration based on changes in the fitness value of the optimal solution. Under normal conditions, DMSGWO gradually reduces the exploration group size while increasing the exploitation group size to enhance local search capability and improve convergence accuracy. Moreover, if the best fitness remains unchanged for three consecutive iterations, the population is considered likely trapped in a local optimum, enabling timely response to stagnation. The three-iteration criterion is used because one or two unchanged iterations may only reflect normal temporary pauses, while waiting for more iterations would delay adjustments. In such cases, the exploration group size is incrementally increased while the exploitation group size is decreased to enhance the algorithm’s ability to escape local optima. Through this strategy, DMSGWO can adaptively adjust the size ratio between exploration and exploitation groups at different iteration stages.

Random position update strategy for exploration group

In GWO, individual position updates rely entirely on the combined guidance of the three leading wolves. However, in complex optimization problems, such guided convergence can easily lead to premature population clustering and entrapment in local optima. To enhance the algorithm’s global search capability, DMSGWO designs a random position update strategy for individuals within the exploration group. The position update direction in this strategy is determined either by a Lévy flight perturbation based on the optimal solution or by a random individual differential perturbation. This approach reduces reliance on the three leading wolves and enables more effective exploration of potential optimal solution regions.

During each iteration, DMSGWO uses a probability threshold of 0.5 to randomly select one of two methods from Eq. (10) for each individual in the exploration group. This threshold gives the two position update directions equal probabilities, preventing the algorithm from biasing toward either one. If the randomly generated probability \(\:p>0.5\), the individual performs a Lévy flight perturbation update. The update direction is perturbed based on the difference between the current individual’s position and the position of the optimal α wolf to explore regions distinct from the current best solution. Lévy flight exhibits heavy-tailed characteristics. In most cases, it generates short step lengths, which are beneficial for fine-grained searches near the individual. Occasionally, it produces extremely long jump steps, which allow the individual to achieve long-distance leaps. This enhances the algorithm’s ability to escape local optima and explores unknown regions significantly. If the random probability \(\:p\le\:0.5\), the individual adopts a random individual differential perturbation update. This mechanism randomly selects two individuals from the current entire population and uses their positional difference as the update direction. The individual’s position update direction is entirely determined by these two randomly chosen individuals, which make the search direction highly randomized and free from attraction to the current optimal solution region. This effectively promotes random information exchange among different individuals within the population, thereby increasing population diversity.

$${X_{new}}(t+1)=\left\{ \begin{gathered} X(t)+rand \cdot \left( {X(t) - {X_\alpha }(t)} \right) \cdot Levy(s){\text{ , }}p>0.5 \hfill \\ X(t)+rand \cdot \left( {{X_1}(t) - {X_2}(t)} \right){\text{ , }}otherwise \hfill \\ \end{gathered} \right.$$
(10)

Here, both \(\:p\) and \(\:rand\) are random numbers within the interval [0, 1]. \(\:{X}_{\alpha\:}\left(t\right)\) denotes the position of the current optimal individual, the α wolf. \(\:{X}_{1}\left(t\right)\) and \(\:{X}_{2}\left(t\right)\) represent the positions of two individuals randomly selected from the current population. The step size \(\:s\) of Lévy flight follows the distribution \(\:\text{L}\stackrel{{\prime }}{\text{e}}\text{vy}\left(\beta\:\right)\), with \(\:\beta\:\) set to 1.5. This value is commonly used in swarm intelligence optimization because it can generate a typical heavy-tailed search pattern, in which most steps are short-distance moves and a few steps are long-distance jumps. Therefore, β = 1.5 helps maintain local search continuity while preserving the ability to escape local optima and explore distant regions of the search space. Specifically, \(\:s=\frac{u}{\mid\:v{\mid\:}^{1/\beta\:}}\), where \(\:u\sim\:N\left(0,{\sigma\:}^{2}\right)\), \(\:v\sim\:N\left(\text{0,1}\right)\), and \(\:\sigma\:={\left[\frac{{\Gamma\:}\left(1+\beta\:\right)\cdot\:\text{s}\text{i}\text{n}\left(\pi\:\beta\:/2\right)}{{\Gamma\:}\left(\frac{1+\beta\:}{2}\right)\cdot\:\beta\:\cdot\:{2}^{\frac{\beta\:-1}{2}}}\right]}^{1/\beta\:}\). Here, \(\:{\Gamma\:}\left(\cdot\:\right)\) denotes the Gamma function.

Finally, the fitness value \(\:f\left({X}_{\text{new}}\left(t+1\right)\right)\) of the new position obtained through the random position update strategy is compared with the fitness value \(\:f\left(X\left(t\right)\right)\) of the original position. If the newly explored position \(\:{X}_{\text{new}}\left(t+1\right)\) is better than the original position \(\:X\left(t\right)\), the new position is selected for updating. Otherwise, the position remains unchanged. This greedy selection mechanism ensures that the overall performance of the population does not degrade during the exploration process. Its specific form is given by Eq. (11).

$$X(t+1)=\left\{ \begin{gathered} {X_{new}}(t+1){\text{ , }}f\left( {{X_{new}}(t+1)} \right)<f\left( {X(t)} \right) \hfill \\ {\text{ }}X(t){\text{ , }}otherwise \hfill \\ \end{gathered} \right.$$
(11)

Adaptive perturbation position update strategy for exploitation group

In GWO, individual position updates assign equal weight to the three leading wolves, causing all individuals to converge toward the same central region. This does not allow for differentiated fine-grained searches based on the varying quality of different individuals’ positions within the search space. Additionally, exploitation behavior is entirely controlled by the convergence factor \(\:a\). It lacks adaptive adjustment capabilities based on the search state. Once the population falls into a local optimum early in the iteration, it becomes difficult to rely on internal driving forces to actively escape. To overcome these limitations, DMSGWO designs an adaptive perturbation position update strategy for individuals in the exploitation group. This strategy follows the cooperative hunting approach of GWO, where the three leading wolves guide the calculation of an essential target position \(\:X\) using Eq. (8). This ensures that the algorithm inherits the fundamental cooperative pack-hunting framework of grey wolves. Building upon this, the strategy designs a relative fitness factor \(\:\varphi\:\) and an adaptive perturbation factor \(\:w\), as shown in Eqs. (12) and (13), respectively. Finally, the adaptive perturbation position update formula for the exploitation group is derived as Eq. (14).

$$\varphi ={\left( {\frac{{{f_i}}}{{{f_{ave}}}}} \right)^3}$$
(12)
$$w=0.5+0.5\cos \left( {\pi \cdot \frac{t}{T}} \right)$$
(13)
$$X(t+1)=(1 - w) \cdot X+w \cdot X \cdot \varphi$$
(14)

Here, \(\:{f}_{i}\) represents the fitness value of the current individual, and \(\:{f}_{\text{avg}}\) denotes the average fitness value of the population. The relative fitness factor \(\:\varphi\:\) enables differentiated searches among individuals. For individuals with poorer fitness, it amplifies the magnitude of their position updates. This encourages larger leaps to potentially escape local optima. Conversely, for individuals with better fitness, it reduces their update step size for stable exploitation and finer refinement around high-quality solutions. The adaptive perturbation factor \(\:w\) varies adaptively with the iteration number \(\:t\) through a cosine function. In the early stage, a larger \(\:w\) allows the differentiated perturbation \(\:X\bullet\:\varphi\:\) to dominate. This allows the exploitation group to maintain strong exploration and escape capabilities during local search. As iterations progress, \(\:w\) gradually decreases, and the weight of the traditional position update \(\:X\) increases. The algorithm gradually shifts toward a cooperative hunting mode primarily guided by the three leading wolves. This dynamically balances the weights between the traditional position update \(\:X\) and the differentiated perturbation update \(\:X\bullet\:\varphi\:\). Such a strategy effectively mitigates issues in GWO, such as loss of population diversity or premature stagnation in local optima during the later exploitation stage.

Pseudocode of DMSGWO

To enhance the search and optimization capabilities of GWO, this paper builds upon the GWO framework and incorporates four improvement strategies to construct DMSGWO. The framework of the four improvement strategies is illustrated in Fig. 5.

DMSGWO first randomly initializes a population of \(\:N\) individuals and evenly divides the initial population into an exploration group \(\:{N}_{1}\) and an exploitation group \(\:{N}_{2}\). Then, the iterative loop begins. The fitness values of all individuals in the current population are calculated. Based on these fitness values, the three best individuals are selected and updated. Next, the nonlinear convergence factor \(\:a\) is computed, and group-based position updates are performed. The individuals in the exploration group undergo the random position update strategy, while those in the exploitation group undergo the adaptive perturbation position update strategy. Under normal circumstances, the algorithm gradually reduces the number of individuals in the exploration group and increases the number in the exploitation group. However, if the optimal solution remains unchanged for three consecutive iterations, a reverse adjustment is triggered to adjust the population structure dynamically. Finally, when the iteration number reaches the preset maximum, the algorithm exits the loop and records the global optimal solution. Time complexity analysis

In the evaluation of algorithm performance, time complexity is a key metric for measuring algorithm efficiency, and describing how the algorithm’s execution time scales with the problem size. The time complexity of GWO is \(\:O\left(T\times\:N\times\:D\right)\), where \(\:T\), \(\:N\), and \(\:D\) represent the maximum number of iterations, population size, and dimensionality, respectively33. The time complexity of the DMSGWO algorithm is analyzed as follows.

First, in the population initialization phase, the initialization procedure of DMSGWO is the same as that of GWO. It randomly generates the initial population for \(\:N\) individuals within the \(\:D\)-dimensional search space, with a time complexity of \(\:O\left(N\times\:D\right)\). Second, during the iterative process, DMSGWO calculates the fitness values for the \(\:N\) individuals and selects the top three optimal individuals in the same way as GWO, with a time complexity of \(\:O\left(N\times\:D\right)\). Additionally, the time complexity of introducing the nonlinear convergence factor strategy in DMSGWO is \(\:O\left(1\right)\), while that of the linear convergence factor in GWO is also \(\:O\left(1\right)\). Finally, the population dynamic grouping strategy in DMSGWO has a time complexity of \(\:O\left(1\right)\). The time complexity of the random position update strategy for \(\:{N}_{1}\) individuals in the exploration group is \(\:O\left({N}_{1}\times\:D\right)\), and the time complexity of the adaptive perturbation position update strategy for \(\:{N}_{2}\) individuals in the exploitation group is \(\:O\left({N}_{2}\times\:D\right)\). In contrast, GWO requires equal-weight position updates for all \(\:N\) individuals in the population, with a time complexity of \(\:O\left(N\times\:D\right)\). Thus, in summary, since \(\:{N=N}_{1}+{N}_{2}\), the overall time complexity of DMSGWO over \(\:T\) iterations is the same as that of GWO: \(\:{O}_{DMSGWO}\left(T\times\:({N}_{1}+{N}_{2})\times\:D\right)={O}_{GWO}\left(T\times\:N\times\:D\right)\).

Experimental design and result analysis

Experimental setup

Comparative algorithms and test functions

To validate the performance of DMSGWO, it is compared with GWO, four other swarm intelligence optimization algorithms and four other improved GWO algorithms: Whale Optimization Algorithm (WOA)6, Harris Hawks Optimization (HHO)34, Black-winged Kite Algorithm (BKA)35, LSHADE_EpSin36, MSGWO37, ISGWO38, BAGWO39, MYIGWO40. Performance testing is conducted on 23 benchmark functions8. Among these, \(\:{F}_{1}\sim{F}_{7}\) are unimodal functions, which have only one global optimum and no local optima. They are used to verify the algorithm’s search accuracy and convergence speed. \(\:{F}_{8}\sim{F}_{13}\) are multimodal functions, which have numerous local optima in the search space. These functions are employed to evaluate the algorithm’s ability to escape local optima and explore the broader search space41. \(\:{F}_{14}\sim{F}_{23}\) are fixed-dimension multimodal functions with low and fixed dimensionality. These simulate the complexity of real-world problems to validate algorithm effectiveness and stability42. Parameters for all compared algorithms are set according to their original references, and the search dimensions for unimodal and multimodal functions among the 23 benchmark functions are set to 30, 100 and 500, respectively. Due to space limitations, experiments with 100 and 500 dimensions are presented in the Supplementary Information.

To further verify that DMSGWO possesses good generalization capability and robustness in complex solution spaces, the CEC2022 test suite43 is used for validation. Specifically, \(\:{F}_{1}\) is a unimodal function, \(\:{F}_{2}\sim{F}_{5}\) are multimodal functions, \(\:{F}_{6}\sim{F}_{8}\) are hybrid functions, and \(\:{F}_{9}\sim{F}_{12}\) are composition functions. The search range for all functions is set to [-100, 100] with search dimensions configured as 10 or 20. Due to space limitations, experiments with 20 dimensions are presented in the Supplementary Information.

Experimental environment and parameters

To ensure a fair comparison of all algorithms, the experiments were conducted using MATLAB R2024a on a computer with an 13th Gen Intel(R) Core (TM) i7-13650HX (2.60 GHz) processor. The parameters were consistent across all runs: a population size of 30 and a maximum of 500 iterations. Each algorithm was executed independently 30 times to reduce randomness. The optimal value (Best) was recorded to represent the theoretical optimal performance of the algorithm. The average value (Avg) was used to measure the overall optimization accuracy and average convergence level. The standard value (Std) was employed to evaluate the stability and robustness of the algorithm. The best results are highlighted in bold.

Ablation experiments and result analysis

To verify the effectiveness of the four proposed improvement strategies and further investigate the internal design mechanism of the algorithm, ablation experiments were conducted in 30 dimensions to clearly demonstrate the contribution of each strategy. DMSGWO_1 denotes the variant that only introduces the nonlinear convergence factor. DMSGWO_23 incorporates the dynamic population grouping strategy and applies the random position update strategy only to the exploration group. DMSGWO_24 incorporates the dynamic population grouping strategy and applies the adaptive perturbation position update strategy only to the exploitation group. DMSGWO_123 adds the nonlinear convergence factor to DMSGWO_23, while DMSGWO_124 adds the nonlinear convergence factor to DMSGWO_24. DMSGWO_234 introduces dynamic population grouping, applies random position update strategy to the exploration group, and applies adaptive perturbation position update strategy to the exploitation group.

According to the ablation results in Table 1, each improvement strategy achieves a significant performance enhancement compared with the original GWO. DMSGWO_1, which only introduces the nonlinear convergence factor, improves the performance of GWO on most functions, indicating that this strategy can better balance exploration and exploitation and alleviate the premature convergence caused by the original linear convergence factor. However, its improvement is still limited, suggesting that the optimization capability of a single strategy has inherent limitations. The comparisons of DMSGWO_123 with DMSGWO_23, DMSGWO_124 with DMSGWO_24, and DMSGWO with DMSGWO_234 further demonstrate the positive contribution of the nonlinear convergence factor to algorithm performance. The results of DMSGWO_23 show that the random position update strategy for the exploration group can improve the algorithm’s performance on multimodal functions, especially on fixed-dimensional multimodal functions, where it exhibits strong global search ability and effectively escapes local optima. However, its local exploitation capability on unimodal functions remains insufficient. In contrast, DMSGWO_24 shows that the adaptive perturbation position update strategy for the exploitation group provides high solution accuracy and stability on unimodal functions and some multimodal functions, although its exploration diversity may be limited. DMSGWO_234 compensates for the limitations of DMSGWO_23 and DMSGWO_24 and achieves a substantial performance improvement on most functions. This indicates that targeted improvements to both the exploration and exploitation groups can generate a strong synergistic effect, enabling the algorithm to possess both strong global exploration capability and refined local exploitation ability.

To verify the significance of each strategy in DMSGWO, a Friedman rank test was conducted based on the results of 30 independent runs of all algorithms to evaluate their overall differences. The Friedman test was used to comprehensively analyze the performance of all algorithms on 23 benchmark functions at a 5% significance level. When the p-value is less than 0.05, the null hypothesis is rejected, indicating statistically significant performance differences among the compared algorithms. After confirming the existence of significant differences, the algorithms were ranked according to their performance on each test function, and the average rank of each algorithm across all functions was calculated. The algorithm with the best overall rank was considered to have the best comprehensive performance. Here, “Ave-Rank” represents the average rank and “Overall-Rank” indicates the comprehensive ranking. The Friedman ranking results show that DMSGWO_234 ranks second overall, while DMSGWO, which further incorporates the nonlinear convergence factor strategy, achieves the best overall performance and ranks first. In addition, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was employed to conduct pairwise statistical comparisons between DMSGWO and the compared algorithms. Here, “+” indicates that the performance of DMSGWO is significantly superior to that of the compared algorithm. “-” indicates the opposite of “+”, whereas “=” indicates no significant difference in performance between the two44. Although the Wilcoxon signed-rank test shows no statistically significant difference between DMSGWO and DMSGWO_234 on most benchmark functions, the slight advantage of DMSGWO in the overall ranking suggests that the nonlinear convergence factor further refines the dynamic regulation of the search process. This enables the algorithm to achieve more balanced and stable performance across different types of functions, ultimately leading to better comprehensive performance. These results demonstrate the progressive contribution and integrated effectiveness of the four improvement strategies in enhancing the overall performance of DMSGWO.

Exploration and exploitation analysis

In the field of optimization algorithms, there exists a common underlying principle: the balance between exploration and exploitation45. A high-quality swarm intelligence optimization algorithm must skillfully navigate this balance. Excessive exploration leads to slow convergence, while excessive exploitation causes premature convergence to local optima. To address this, we use Eq. (15) to calculate the exploration rate and Eq. (16) for the exploitation rate, which more accurately characterize the algorithm’s exploration and exploitation process during execution.

$$Exploration(\% )=\frac{{Div(t)}}{{Di{v_{\hbox{max} }}}} \times 100$$
(15)
$$Exploitation(\% )=\frac{{\left| {Div(t) - Di{v_{\hbox{max} }}} \right|}}{{Di{v_{\hbox{max} }}}} \times 100$$
(16)
$$Div(t)=\frac{1}{{Dim}}\sum\limits_{{d=1}}^{{Dim}} {\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{{i=1}}^{N} {\left| {median\left( {{x_d}(t)} \right) - {x_{id}}(t)} \right|} }$$
(17)

where \(\:t\) is the current iteration number, \(\:\text{Dim}\) is the problem dimensionality, and \(\:d\) is the current dimension index. \(\:N\) represents the population size, and \(\:i\) denotes the current individual. \(\:{x}_{id}\left(t\right)\) is the value of the \(\:i\)-th individual in the \(\:d\)-th dimension at iteration \(\:t\). \(\:\text{median}\text{(}{x}_{id})\) is the median of all individuals’ values in the \(\:d\)-th dimension at iteration \(\:t\). \(\:\text{Div}\left(t\right)\) indicates the population diversity at iteration \(\:t\), and \(\:{\text{Div}}_{\text{m}\text{a}\text{x}}\) is the maximum population diversity observed throughout the entire iteration.

The legend specifies the line colors for exploration and exploitation, as well as the average exploration and exploitation rates during the iteration process. From the figure, the average exploration rate of GWO is low, indicating that the algorithm shifts to exploitation too early with insufficient exploration, thus limiting its global optimization capability. In contrast, DMSGWO significantly extends the exploration phase through its four improvement strategies. DMSGWO achieves more comprehensive global exploration in the early stage and enables a smooth transition to the local exploitation stage, effectively mitigating the issue of excessive exploration in GWO and achieving a better balance between exploration and exploitation.

Diversity Analysis

Population diversity is an important indicator for evaluating the distribution characteristics of search agents during the optimization process46. A larger diversity value indicates that individuals are more widely distributed in the search space, which is beneficial for global exploration, whereas a smaller diversity value means that the population is concentrated around promising regions, reflecting stronger local exploitation. Considering that different benchmark functions have different dimensions and search ranges, this study adopts a normalized population diversity metric to improve the comparability of diversity curves across functions. At iteration \(\:t\), the population center in the \(\:k\)-th dimension \(\:{\stackrel{\prime }{x}}_{k}\left(t\right)\), the population radius \(\:R\left(t\right)\), and the normalized diversity \(\:Div\left(t\right)\) are calculated as follows:

$${\overline {x} _k}=\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{{i=1}}^{N} {{x_{i,k}}\left( t \right)}$$
(18)
$$R\left( t \right)=\mathop {\hbox{max} }\limits_{{p \ne q}} \sqrt {\sum\limits_{{k=1}}^{d} {{{\left[ {{x_{p,k}}\left( t \right) - {x_{q,k}}\left( t \right)} \right]}^2}} }$$
(19)
$$Div\left( t \right)=\frac{1}{{N \cdot R\left( t \right)}}\sum\limits_{{i=1}}^{N} {\sqrt {\sum\limits_{{k=1}}^{d} {{{\left[ {{x_{i,k}}\left( t \right) - {{\overline {x} }_k}\left( t \right)} \right]}^2}} } }$$
(20)

where \(\:N\) is the population size, \(\:d\) is the problem dimension. \(\:{x}_{i,k}\left(t\right)\) denotes the position of the \(\:i\)-th individual in the \(\:k\)-th dimension at iteration \(\:t\), where \(\:i=\text{1,2},\dots\:,N\) and \(\:k=\text{1,2},\dots\:,d\). \(\:{\stackrel{\prime }{x}}_{k}\left(t\right)\) represents the mean position of all individuals in the \(\:k\)-th dimension. \(\:p,q\in\:\{\text{1,2},\dots\:,N\}\) and \(\:p\ne\:q\) are two different individual indices used to calculate the maximum pairwise Euclidean distance within the population. A larger \(\:Div\left(t\right)\) indicates stronger population dispersion, while a smaller \(\:Div\left(t\right)\) indicates stronger population concentration.

Figure 7 shows normalized diversity curves of DMSGWO and GWO on the 30-dimensional functions \(\:{F}_{1}\), \(\:{F}_{4}\), \(\:{F}_{9}\) and \(\:{F}_{11}\), as well as \(\:{F}_{19}\) and \(\:{F}_{23}\). Overall, although GWO maintains relatively high diversity, it lacks an effective convergence control mechanism, which may reduce search efficiency. In contrast, DMSGWO achieves a better balance between exploration and exploitation. For \(\:{F}_{1}\) and \(\:{F}_{4}\), the diversity of DMSGWO decreases rapidly, indicating that the nonlinear factor strategy and dynamic population grouping strategy reduce unnecessary dispersion, thereby accelerating convergence to the optimum. On \(\:{F}_{9}\) and \(\:{F}_{11}\), DMSGWO can moderately recover diversity after convergence. This indicates that the exploration group and the stagnation feedback mechanism in dynamic grouping can restore exploration ability when necessary, improving the probability of escaping local optima. On \(\:{F}_{19}\) and \(\:{F}_{23}\), the diversity of DMSGWO increases in the early stage and gradually decreases in the middle and later stages. This suggests that the algorithm first enhances population diversity through the exploration group, and then guides the population toward promising regions through the exploitation group and the nonlinear convergence factor.

Application of DMSGWO to WSN coverage optimization problem

Wireless Sensor Networks (WSN) are distributed sensing networks featuring low power consumption, low cost, wide coverage, and functional integration53. They are widely used in smart healthcare, urban traffic management, agricultural monitoring, and other fields, and are considered a key technology for future human life54. However, sensor nodes have limited energy and are often randomly scattered in harsh monitoring environments, leading to coverage holes in some areas and excessive overlap in others. This degrades both monitoring quality and network reliability55. Therefore, adaptive deployment of nodes to improve distribution uniformity and increase coverage is critical56.

The 2D WSN coverage optimization problem is defined as follows57. In a monitoring area of size \(\:M\times\:N\), \(\:n\) sensor nodes \(\:S=\left\{{s}_{1},\:{s}_{2},\cdots\:,{s}_{n}\right\}\)are randomly deployed, each with the same sensing radius \(\:R\), to maximize coverage. To simplify the problem, the area is discretized into \(\:M\times\:N\) grids, with each grid center as a target point \(\:O=\left\{{o}_{1},\:{o}_{2},\cdots\:,{o}_{M\times\:N}\right\}\). The distance \(\:d\) between sensor node \(\:{s}_{i}\left({x}_{i},{y}_{i}\right)\) and target point \(\:{o}_{j}\left({x}_{j},{y}_{j}\right)\) is expressed as:

$$d({s_i},{o_j})=\sqrt {{{({x_i} - {x_j})}^2}+{{({y_i} - {y_j})}^2}}$$
(27)

Using the Boolean sensing model, if \(\:d({s}_{i},{p}_{j})\le\:{R}_{s}\), then \(\:{o}_{j}\) is covered by \(\:{s}_{i}\) with perception probability \(\:P=1\); otherwise, \(\:P=0\):

$$P({s_i},{o_j})=\left\{ \begin{gathered} 1{\text{ , }}d({s_i},{o_j}) \leqslant {R_s} \\ 0{\text{ , }}otherwise \\ \end{gathered} \right.$$
(28)

In the monitoring area, each target point may be covered by multiple sensor nodes simultaneously. The joint sensing probability \(\:{C}_{p}\) of target point \(\:{o}_{j}\) covered by all sensor nodes \(\:S\) is:

$${C_p}(S,{o_j})=1 - \prod\limits_{{i=1}}^{n} {\left( {1 - P({s_i},{o_j})} \right)}$$
(29)

Using the maximization of coverage rate \(\:{P}_{\text{cov}}\) as the fitness function, DMSGWO is applied to solve the WSN coverage optimization problem.

$${P_{\operatorname{cov} }}=\frac{{\sum\limits_{{j=1}}^{{M \times N}} {{C_p}(S,{o_j})} }}{{M \times N}}$$
(30)

In this section, the 2D monitoring area is \(\:100\times\:100{\hspace{0.17em}}{\text{m}}^{2}\), sensor nodes have a sensing radius of \(\:12{\hspace{0.17em}m}\), and the number of nodes is set to 10, 20, and 30, respectively. The comparison algorithms, experimental environment, and parameters are the same as in Section “Experimental Design and Result Analysis”. Performance is evaluated using best coverage (Best), average coverage (Avg), and standard deviation (Std).

Based on the WSN coverage results in Table 6, the WSN coverage rate of all algorithms increases significantly as the number of sensor nodes increases from 10 to 20 and 30, indicating that increasing the number of sensors can effectively improve the coverage performance in the two-dimensional monitoring area. Although the best coverage rates of DMSGWO with 20 and 30 sensor nodes are slightly lower than those of LSHADE_EpSin, DMSGWO achieves the highest average coverage rate and the lowest standard deviation in all three cases. This indicates that DMSGWO not only obtains competitive coverage optimization results, but also exhibits better stability and robustness. As shown in the sensor coverage deployment diagrams in Fig. 12, Supplementary Fig. S10 and Supplementary Fig. S11, the sensor nodes deployed by most comparison algorithms are excessively clustered within the two-dimensional monitoring area, whereas the sensor nodes deployed by DMSGWO are distributed more uniformly. As shown in the convergence curve comparison of WSN coverage in Fig. 13, DMSGWO outperforms the comparison algorithms in both solution accuracy and convergence speed. This demonstrates that DMSGWO achieves high solution accuracy, fast convergence and uniform distribution when solving the two-dimensional WSN coverage optimization problem. It can quickly and effectively improve network coverage, thereby reducing network construction costs.

Conclusion

This paper conducts systematic research on the performance improvement of the GWO and its application expansion. Firstly, to address the shortcomings of the standard GWO, DMSGWO is proposed by integrating four innovative improvement strategies. Secondly, in terms of algorithm performance, DMSGWO is evaluated using 23 benchmark test functions and the authoritative CEC2022 test suite. Experimental results demonstrate that DMSGWO exhibits significant advantages in solution accuracy, convergence speed, and stability compared to other comparative algorithms. DMSGWO ranks first in the overall algorithm ranking. Finally, in terms of application expansion, DMSGWO is applied to two typical engineering design optimization problems and WSN coverage optimization problem. These results verify its effectiveness and practicality in constrained optimization problems. Future work can further optimize the algorithm and expand its application to other types of optimization problem domains.
